By Imraan, freeabtest.com. Published 31 October 2026.

The SaaS landing page tests that need the least traffic are big changes measured on a click close to the change: rewriting the headline around one outcome, measured on clicks of the main button, needs about 4,552 visitors in total to detect a 30% lift on an 8% click rate. A button color change measured the same way needs about 147,706 visitors to detect a 5% lift, so most small sites should skip it.

Most lists of A/B test ideas treat every idea as equal. They are not. The visitors a test needs depend on two things: how often the goal happens, and how big a change the idea can plausibly make. Below are 25 ideas for a SaaS landing page, ranked from the fewest visitors needed to the most, with the numbers we calculated for each and the plan limits that apply if you run them on freeabtest.com.

## How did we rank these ideas?

We gave every idea two inputs, then calculated the sample size with the standard two-proportion formula at 95% confidence and 80% power, two-sided, with a 50/50 split. It is the same formula behind [Evan Miller's sample size calculator](https://www.evanmiller.org/ab-testing/sample-size.html) and our article on [A/B test sample size](https://www.freeabtest.com/blog/ab-test-sample-size).

The first input is the goal, with an example baseline. A baseline is the conversion rate your page gets today on that goal. We used four example rates for a SaaS landing page. Replace them with your own numbers from the last few weeks before you plan anything.

| Goal | Example baseline | 10% lift | 20% lift | 30% lift |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Click on the main button | 8% | 18,872 | 4,921 | 2,276 |
| Click on a secondary link, such as "See pricing" | 4% | 39,475 | 10,317 | 4,783 |
| Completed signup form | 3% | 53,211 | 13,914 | 6,455 |
| Completed demo request | 1.5% | 108,153 | 28,304 | 13,141 |

The cells are visitors per version, so a plain A/B test needs twice that number.

The second input is the lift we would plan for. A planning lift (the minimum detectable effect) is the smallest relative change the test is set up to detect. It is not a prediction. We used one rule for every idea:

- **30%** when the idea changes the offer, the first thing a visitor reads, or the whole page.
- **20%** when it adds or removes a piece of information or an option that visitors use to decide.
- **10%** when it rewords or rearranges something that is already there.
- **5%** when the change is cosmetic.

These are planning choices, and they lean optimistic. Big real effects are rare. In a [2017 meta-analysis of more than 6,700 e-commerce experiments](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/advertising/2017-browne.pdf), Will Browne and Mike Swarbrick Jones of Qubit found that 90% of experiments moved revenue by less than 1.2% either way. Kohavi and Thomke reported in [Harvard Business Review](https://hbr.org/2017/09/the-surprising-power-of-online-experiments) that only about 10% to 20% of experiments at Google and Bing produce positive results. Planning for a 30% lift does not make one happen. It tells you the smallest effect your traffic can see.

## Which landing page tests need the least traffic?

Ideas 1 to 4 change the offer or the first thing visitors read. Measured on clicks of the main button at 8%, each needs about 2,276 visitors per version, or 4,552 in total. At 2,500 visitors a week to the page, that is 2 weeks, the minimum we recommend so both weekdays and weekends are counted.

1. **Outcome headline instead of a feature headline.** Test "Know which page converts better in a week" against "A/B testing with live stats and click goals". The headline is the one thing almost every visitor reads, which is why it can move the button click rate by a lot in either direction.
2. **"Start free" instead of "Book a demo" as the main call to action.** This changes what you are offering, not just the words. Measure button clicks, then compare completed signups against demo requests before you ship, because more clicks on a lower-commitment button can still mean fewer customers.
3. **Show prices instead of "Contact us for pricing".** Visitors who cannot see a price often leave to find one. Measure clicks on the main button and, on paid plans, visits to the signup page as a second goal.
4. **Short page against long page.** Cut the page to the hero, 3 benefits, one proof point and the button, and test it against the full page. A big structural change like this is easiest to build as a [split URL test](https://www.freeabtest.com/blog/split-url-testing), where the variant lives at its own URL.

## Which tests fit 2,500 to 10,000 visitors a week?

Ideas 5 to 12 add or remove one piece of information or one option. At a 20% planning lift on an 8% click rate, each needs 4,921 visitors per version, or 9,842 in total. That is 4 weeks at 2,500 visitors a week, or 2 weeks at 10,000.

5. **Customer logos above the fold.** Put 4 to 6 recognizable customer logos directly under the hero. Use only customers who agreed to be shown.
6. **"No credit card required" under the button.** One line of microcopy that answers the most common reason not to click a trial button. Only test it if it is true.
7. **A named testimonial with a photo next to the button.** A quote with a full name, role and company, placed where the visitor decides, instead of in a carousel further down.
8. **An objection FAQ above the final call to action.** Three short answers to what stops people signing up, such as data privacy, setup time and cancelling.
9. **One call to action instead of two.** Test a page with only "Start free" against one with "Start free" and "Book a demo" side by side. Removing a choice can raise clicks on the one that is left, or lose the visitors who wanted the other.
10. **No top navigation on ad landing pages.** Visitors from paid ads arrive for one reason. Removing the menu cuts the exits. Keep a way back to the homepage in the footer.
11. **A product screenshot instead of an illustration.** A real screenshot of the product in the hero shows what visitors get. Abstract illustrations show nothing they can judge.
12. **A subheadline with a specific number.** "Set up in 15 minutes" or "Used on 1,200 sites" instead of a general claim, as long as the number is real and you can stand behind it.

## Which tests should wait until you have more traffic?

Ideas 13 to 21 need between 12,910 and 37,744 visitors in total, either because they are measured on a rarer goal or because they reword something already on the page.

13. **Email-only signup instead of a full form (12,910 visitors).** Ask for an email address first and the rest after signup. This is measured on completed signups at 3%, which is why it needs more traffic than a headline test with the same 30% planning lift.
14. **A "Plans from $X a month" line under the button (20,634).** Measured on clicks of the "See pricing" link at 4%. It answers the price question without sending visitors away from the page.
15. **Fewer fields on the demo request form (26,282).** Drop company size and phone number, for example. Demo requests at 1.5% are the rarest goal in this list, so even a 30% planning lift needs a lot of traffic.
16. **Security and privacy badges next to the signup form (27,828).** Only show badges for audits or certifications you actually hold.
17. **Button copy: "Start free trial" against "Get started" (37,744).** Rewording the button is one of the most popular tests and one of the hardest to read. Qubit's meta-analysis found that changing call to action wording averaged -0.3% on revenue per visitor.
18. **A short demo video instead of a static screenshot (37,744).** Keep it under a minute and make sure the page still works when the video does not play.
19. **A sticky call to action bar on mobile (37,744).** Keeps the button on screen as visitors scroll. Check that it does not cover content or the cookie banner.
20. **Repeat the call to action after each section (37,744).** Puts a button where a long-page reader makes the decision, rather than only at the top and bottom.
21. **Most used feature first (37,744).** Reorder the features section so the one customers use most comes first.

## Which landing page tests are rarely worth running?

Ideas 22 to 25 are cosmetic. At a 5% planning lift on an 8% click rate, each needs 73,853 visitors per version, or 147,706 in total. That is 60 weeks at 2,500 visitors a week and 15 weeks at 10,000.

22. **Button color.** Qubit found color changes averaged a 0.0% effect on revenue per visitor across the experiments they analyzed, and recommended choosing a design and sticking with it.
23. **Button size or shape.** Button changes averaged -0.2% in the same analysis.
24. **Hero photo swap.** One stock photo for another.
25. **Font change.** A new typeface or weight for headings.

If a cosmetic change is part of a redesign you want anyway, ship it and move on. Spend your test slots on ideas 1 to 12.

## How big a lift can your traffic detect?

Work backwards from the visitors you have. This table shows the smallest relative lift a 4-week test with two versions can detect, at 95% confidence and 80% power:

| Visitors a week to the page | Button click (8%) | Pricing link click (4%) | Signup (3%) | Demo request (1.5%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1,000 | 32.1% | 48.1% | 56.8% | 85.5% |
| 2,500 | 19.8% | 29.3% | 34.4% | 50.7% |
| 5,000 | 13.9% | 20.3% | 23.8% | 34.7% |
| 10,000 | 9.7% | 14.2% | 16.6% | 24.0% |
| 25,000 | 6.1% | 8.9% | 10.3% | 14.9% |
| 50,000 | 4.3% | 6.2% | 7.2% | 10.4% |

If your row says 32% for button clicks, ideas 1 to 4 are realistic and the rest are not, at least not on this page. ## What makes a landing page test result trustworthy?

- **Decide the goal and planning lift before launch.** Write them in the test's hypothesis. Changing the goal after you see the data turns a test into a search for a winner.
- **Check the next step before you ship.** A variant that raises button clicks but lowers completed signups is a loss.
- **Run whole weeks.** Paid campaigns, weekday traffic and weekend traffic convert differently.
- **Watch for a sample ratio mismatch.** A 50/50 test that ends at 54/46 usually has a bug in the redirect or the tracking. freeabtest.com shows a warning on every plan.
- **Read the interval, not only the winner.** A made-up example: "button clicks went from 8.0% to 9.6%, and the lift could be anywhere from 1% to 38%" tells you far more than "B wins". Our explainer on [statistical significance](https://www.freeabtest.com/blog/statistical-significance-ab-test) shows how to read it.
- **Do not fake urgency or scarcity.** Countdown timers that reset and "only 3 seats left" on software with unlimited seats can win a test and still cost you trust.

## Frequently asked questions

**What should I A/B test first on a SaaS landing page?**
Test the change with the biggest likely effect, measured on the goal closest to it. A headline rewrite or a switch from "Book a demo" to "Start free", measured on clicks of the main button at an 8% click rate, needs about 4,552 visitors for a 30% lift. A button color change measured the same way needs about 147,706 for a 5% lift.

**How much traffic do I need to A/B test a landing page?**
It depends on the goal's conversion rate and the lift you want to detect. In 4 weeks, 2,500 visitors a week can detect about a 20% lift on an 8% button click rate, and 10,000 a week can detect about 10%. Detecting a 5% lift on the same goal takes about 147,706 visitors in total.

**Is it worth A/B testing button colors?**
Rarely. In Qubit's 2017 meta-analysis of more than 6,700 e-commerce experiments, color changes averaged a 0.0% effect on revenue per visitor and button changes averaged -0.2%. Planning for a 5% lift on an 8% click rate needs about 73,853 visitors per version, which most small SaaS sites will not reach in a reasonable time.

**Should I measure clicks or signups in a landing page test?**
Measure the goal closest to the change, and check the later goal before you ship. A click on the main button at 8% needs about 2,276 visitors per version for a 30% lift, while completed signups at 3% need 6,455 and demo requests at 1.5% need 13,141. More clicks that do not turn into signups are not a win.

**How many landing page tests can I run at once?**
Run one test per page at a time, so you know which change moved the number. On freeabtest.com's Free plan you get 1 running test and 1 new test a month, with 10,000 tested visitors a month. Starter allows 20 running tests and Growth allows unlimited.

**What percentage of A/B tests win?**
Fewer than most people expect. Ron Kohavi and Stefan Thomke wrote in Harvard Business Review in 2017 that only about 10% to 20% of experiments at Google and Bing produce positive results, and that at Microsoft as a whole about one third are positive, one third neutral and one third negative. Plan for most ideas to be flat.

We calculated every number in this article with a short Python script using SciPy's normal distribution and the two-proportion formula, two-sided, at 95% confidence and 80% power with equal groups, rounding each result up to the next whole visitor. The baselines (8%, 4%, 3% and 1.5%) and the planning lifts are example inputs, not results from real tests, and the reverse table searches for the smallest lift whose required sample fits 4 weeks of traffic. Put your own baseline into Evan Miller's calculator or the freeabtest.com Test planner and the ranking will shift, but the order of the tiers rarely does.
